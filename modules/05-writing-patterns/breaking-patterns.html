When to Break the Pattern

This course has taught patterns as if they were fixed rules. They are not. They are learning scaffolds, and a scaffold is removed when the building can stand on its own. This last lesson shows a real paragraph where an expert breaks, on purpose, the pattern you learned, and the paragraph works better for it.

The model

We return to the first model of the course: the final sentence of the 1953 Watson and Crick article. Read it again, now with a different question: not what it says, but which move it avoids stating.

“It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material.”
Watson, J. D., & Crick, F. H. C. (1953). Molecular structure of nucleic acids: A structure for deoxyribose nucleic acid. Nature, 171(4356), 737–738. https://doi.org/10.1038/171737a0

Taking it apart

In a strict PEEL paragraph the Link is an explicit sentence. Here the Link is disguised. Label each part.

It has not escaped our notice that
the specific pairing we have postulated
immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material.
Versión PEEL estricta posible: «We have discovered the mechanism by which genetic material is copied», un Link directo, casi un titular.

Why this works

According to the lesson, why does the Watson and Crick sentence work better than a triumphant, explicit Link?

Because patterns are unnecessary for experts.
Because the authors knew the pattern they were breaking, and in 1953 a triumphant Link would have sounded arrogant before anyone had replicated the finding.
Because shorter sentences are always better.
Respuesta: B. «La modestia retórica no debilitó el mensaje, lo hizo más creíble». No es prueba de que los patrones sobren; es prueba de que hay que dominarlos primero.

Rebuild it: the strict version

“It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material.” (Watson & Crick, 1953)

Rewrite this sentence as a strict PEEL Link: explicit, direct, without litotes. Then, in one sentence, say what is gained in clarity and what is lost in rhetorical effect.

The rewritten sentence is an explicit, direct Link with no litotes and no distancing frame (for example, “We have discovered that DNA replicates by copying each strand using complementary base pairing.”).
The reflection names what is gained (immediate clarity) and what is lost (the rhetorical modesty and the effect of letting the reader complete the implication).
Modelo (Apéndice C): «We have discovered that DNA replicates by copying each strand using complementary base pairing.» Se gana claridad inmediata; se pierde la modestia retórica del original.

Four questions before you break a pattern

This lesson has no phrase bank. Instead, there are four questions you should be able to answer every time you decide to break a pattern from this course:

  1. Can I write the version that follows the pattern to the letter? If I cannot, I am not breaking the pattern: I am avoiding it.
  2. What does my reader gain from the break that they would not gain from the strict version?
  3. Does the convention of my discipline or journal tolerate this break, or penalise it?
  4. Could I justify this decision in front of a professor, a reviewer or an editor?

The debate: critics of formulaic writing, such as Wesley (2000) in The Ill Effects of the Five-Paragraph Theme, have noted that teaching a pattern as a rigid formula produces writers who do not know when to drop it. But the Watson and Crick paragraph is not proof that patterns are unnecessary; it is proof that they must be mastered first.

Spot it in the wild

Find, in an award acceptance speech, a letter to the editor or an opinion article from your field, a paragraph that deliberately breaks a pattern you already know.

  1. Which move does the author omit or disguise?
  2. Is the paragraph still understandable without that explicit move? Why?

Not graded. Bring your answers to class and compare them with a classmate.

Now you write

You will write your own paragraph using the Breaking the pattern pattern. Allow about 15 minutes. Your writing is tracked for academic integrity: write it yourself, in your own words.

Take a paragraph you wrote in a previous lesson of this category. Write a second version that deliberately breaks one move of the pattern you used. Below it, write a short paragraph in English that justifies why the break improves the text for your reader and your purpose.

Self-assessment rubric: Breaking the pattern

Before you submit, check your paragraph against each row. You can ask the AI for a quick check (maximum two times): it suggests the marks and you decide. If three or more rows need work, rewrite before you hand it in.

General writing criteria
Academic register: formal tone, no contractions or colloquial expressions.
Vocabulary: precise, specific words instead of vague ones (thing, stuff, a lot of).
Cohesion: connectors and reference words link each sentence to the previous one.
Grammar and mechanics: complete sentences, agreement, punctuation, spelling.
Focus: the whole paragraph develops one main idea.
