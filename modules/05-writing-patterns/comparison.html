Comparison: Block and Alternating

When a text compares two approaches, studies or phenomena it has two structural options. The block scheme presents everything about A and then everything about B. The alternating scheme presents one point of comparison at a time, alternating A and B. Both are legitimate; each fits a different situation.

The model

This passage, adapted from the abstract of the 2015 study on the gender gap, organises a comparison between two types of language skills. Decide whether it follows a block or an alternating scheme.

The gender gap in second language acquisition is not uniform across language modalities. In productive skills, the gap is pronounced: female learners consistently outperformed male learners in speaking and writing proficiency, and this advantage persisted after controlling for education, age of arrival, length of residence, and hours studying Dutch. In receptive skills, the picture is different: for reading and listening, no significant gender difference was found. This contrast suggests that the advantage is tied specifically to the demands of language production rather than to a general cognitive difference between male and female learners.
Adapted from Van der Slik, F. W. P., Van Hout, R. W. N. M., & Schepens, J. J. (2015). The gender gap in second language acquisition: Gender differences in the acquisition of Dutch among immigrants from 88 countries with 49 mother tongues. PLoS ONE, 10(11), e0142056. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0142056

Taking it apart

Label the role of each segment in the comparison.

The gender gap in second language acquisition is not uniform across language modalities.
In productive skills, the gap is pronounced: female learners consistently outperformed male learners in speaking and writing proficiency…
In receptive skills, the picture is different: for reading and listening, no significant gender difference was found.
This contrast suggests that the advantage is tied specifically to the demands of language production…
Esquema alternado: punto por punto. Si el mismo contenido fuese en bloque, el primer párrafo traería todo sobre destrezas productivas y el segundo todo sobre las receptivas, con la síntesis al final.

Block or alternating?

A literature review compares two theories across eight criteria, and the reader needs a complete picture of Theory A before taking in Theory B. Which scheme fits better?

Block: it suits many points of comparison and a reader who needs the full picture of A first.
Alternating: it suits many points because it never repeats the subjects.
Respuesta: A. En bloque conviene cuando hay muchos puntos (revisiones largas); alternado, cuando hay pocos y el interés está en el contraste inmediato.

Rebuild it: from block to alternating

“Explicit vocabulary instruction involves the teacher selecting target words, presenting definitions, and designing practice activities around those words. Students typically learn 8 to 10 words per lesson and retain them well on immediate posttests. Incidental vocabulary acquisition, by contrast, occurs when learners encounter unknown words during extensive reading without direct instruction. The number of words acquired per session is lower, usually 1 to 3, but the learning is embedded in meaningful context and may produce deeper retention over time.”

This paragraph compares two approaches in block format. Rewrite it in alternating format without losing any point of comparison.

The rewrite is organised point by point (how words are presented, words learned per session, retention), treating both explicit instruction and incidental acquisition at each point, with no point lost.
It uses visible comparison connectors (whereas, in contrast, by contrast, unlike) and keeps an academic register.
Solución (Apéndice C): la conversión organiza la comparación punto por punto: cantidad de palabras por sesión en ambos métodos, después la retención, después el contexto, en lugar de todo un método y luego todo el otro.

Spot it in the wild

Find a review article from your field in open access. Read a paragraph where the author compares two methods, two theories or two results.

  1. Does the paragraph follow a block scheme (all A, then all B) or an alternating one (point by point)?
  2. Which connectors does the author use to mark the comparison (similarly, in contrast, whereas, on the other hand)?
  3. Does the comparison end with a synthesis that interprets the contrast, or must the reader draw the conclusion?

Not graded. Bring your answers to class and compare them with a classmate.

Now you write

You will write your own paragraph using the Comparison (block and alternating) pattern. Allow about 15 minutes. Your writing is tracked for academic integrity: write it yourself, in your own words.

Choose two approaches, methods or theories from your field. Write in English a comparative paragraph of six to eight sentences. Decide before writing whether you will use a block or an alternating scheme, and justify your choice in one sentence at the end.

Self-assessment rubric: Comparison (block and alternating)

Before you submit, check your paragraph against each row. You can ask the AI for a quick check (maximum two times): it suggests the marks and you decide. If three or more rows need work, rewrite before you hand it in.

This pattern
I chose block or alternating according to the number of points of comparison.
Each point of comparison appears for both elements, not just one.
I use comparison and contrast connectors (whereas, in contrast, similarly).
My conclusion says what the comparison means; it does not just summarise it.
General writing criteria
Academic register: formal tone, no contractions or colloquial expressions.
Vocabulary: precise, specific words instead of vague ones (thing, stuff, a lot of).
Cohesion: connectors and reference words link each sentence to the previous one.
Grammar and mechanics: complete sentences, agreement, punctuation, spelling.
Focus: the whole paragraph develops one main idea.
