CER: Reporting Results and Data

When a paragraph reports empirical results, PEEL loses its fourth move. CER has only three: Claim, Evidence and Reasoning. The link with the big argument is saved for the Discussion. CER is the default pattern of results sections in science, engineering and quantitative social science.

The model

This paragraph comes from the results section of an open-access study on spaced retrieval practice with Ecuadorian learners of English. Significance and effect size point in different directions, and the Reasoning resolves that tension.

The Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test comparing Target Vocabulary Usage between the pretest and posttest showed an increase in vocabulary integration, with the mean rising from 23.13% (SD = 9.57) to 27.52% (SD = 4.58). The median also increased from 21.08% to 27.37%. However, the difference was not statistically significant (W = 9, p = 0.117). Despite this, the effect size (r = 0.45) is considered medium.
Faytong-Haro, M. (2025). Unlocking words and fluency: Spaced Retrieval small-scale case study practice with Spanish-speaking A1 EFL adult learners. Frontiers in Education, 10, Article 1715111. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2025.1715111

Why CER works in results sections

A reader of a results section wants three things, in this order: what did you find, what are the numbers, and what do the numbers mean.

  • A paragraph that only gives means and p-values is a table disguised as prose: the reader has to do the analytical work.
  • A paragraph that interprets without data is an unsupported opinion.

CER and PEEL are the same wave with a different reach: Claim = Point, Evidence = Evidence, Reasoning = Explanation. The only structural difference is the missing Link, which is a convention of the genre.

Si el párrafo que el estudiante encuentra no tiene Reasoning, es un patrón CE incompleto: ¿la interpretación está en otro párrafo cercano o en la discusión?

Rebuild it: which move is each sentence?

These sentences come from a hypothetical study on spaced retrieval. Drag each one to its CER move.

Learners who practised vocabulary with spaced retrieval produced more target words in the posttest than learners who did not.
Students in the experimental group wrote longer sentences than the control group after the intervention.
The spaced group used a mean of 31.4% target vocabulary (SD = 6.2), compared with 24.8% (SD = 7.1) in the control group, t(28) = 2.74, p = .011.
The experimental group averaged 18.2 words per sentence (SD = 3.1) and the control group 15.9 (SD = 3.6), t(40) = 2.20, p = .034.
This difference suggests that retrieving words at increasing intervals strengthens memory traces more than massed repetition.
The size of this gap indicates that the intervention supported more elaborate sentence construction, not just more words.

Rebuild it: write the Evidence and the Reasoning

Hypothetical study: bilingual versus monolingual dictionaries in an English reading class.

GroupnMeanSD
Bilingual dictionary3274.38.6
Monolingual dictionary3069.110.2
t(60) = 2.19, p = .033, d = 0.56

Claim (given): “Students who used bilingual dictionaries during reading tasks scored higher on the comprehension posttest than students who used monolingual dictionaries.”

Write one sentence of Evidence that reports the data in the table, and one sentence of Reasoning that explains what they mean for the Claim.

The Evidence sentence reports both group means, at least one SD, and the test result (t(60) = 2.19, p = .033, d = 0.56) accurately and concisely.
The Reasoning sentence interprets the data (for example, that access to a first-language equivalent reduces lookup effort or that the effect is medium) instead of repeating the numbers, and uses hedged academic language.
Modelo (Apéndice C): Evidence: «Students who used bilingual dictionaries scored an average of 74.3 (SD = 8.6) on the comprehension posttest, compared to 69.1 (SD = 10.2) for the monolingual dictionary group, t(60) = 2.19, p = .033, d = 0.56.» Reasoning: «This small but significant difference suggests that access to a first language equivalent reduces the cognitive load of lookup, leaving more attentional resources for comprehension itself.»

Spot it in the wild

Find an empirical article from your field in open access and read one paragraph from the Results section. Identify the three CER moves.

  1. Does the first sentence announce a finding (Claim), or does it just report a number with no context?
  2. Does the data include measures of dispersion (SD, CI) and statistical significance (p, d)?
  3. Does the author explain what the data mean for the research question (Reasoning), or does the paragraph end with the last number?

Not graded. Bring your answers to class and compare them with a classmate.

Si falta el Reasoning, observar si aparece en otro párrafo cercano o en la discusión.

Now you write

You will write your own paragraph using the CER (Claim, Evidence, Reasoning) pattern. Allow about 12 minutes. Your writing is tracked for academic integrity: write it yourself, in your own words.

Invent or recall a data set from your field (two groups, two means and a difference) and write a complete CER paragraph in English, three to four sentences. If you have no data of your own, use the table from the previous exercise.
