Toulmin: The Anatomy of an Argument

Stephen Toulmin proposed in 1958 that every argument is built from six pieces. Not all appear in every paragraph, but three are indispensable: a Claim, Grounds and a Warrant. Their absence is the most precise diagnosis of a weak argument.

Why Toulmin works

Toulmin works like an X-ray of the argumentative paragraph. When a PEEL paragraph argues (rather than reporting results), its Explanation is exactly Toulmin’s Warrant. Toulmin simply makes visible the piece PEEL groups under one label.

Three components are indispensable: without a Claim there is no argument; without Grounds there is no proof; without a Warrant there is no connection between the proof and what is claimed. Backing, Qualifier and Rebuttal make the argument more resistant to criticism, but may be absent without the paragraph falling apart.

The most frequent error in student argumentative writing is the missing Warrant: the student asserts something, cites a source and moves on. The reader who does not know the field asks, “and what does that prove?”. That question is the sign that the Warrant is missing.

Qualifier y Rebuttal son herramientas de credibilidad: reaparecen en Concesión (Cap. 10) y Metadiscurso (Cap. 16).

Spot the pattern: classify the sentence

These sentences belong to a hypothetical argument that extensive reading improves reading fluency. Drag each to its Toulmin component.

Nakanishi (2015) conducted a meta-analysis of 34 studies and found a mean effect size of d = 0.71 in favor of extensive reading.
A report on programmes in several countries showed that learners who read extensively improved their reading speed.
This is likely because extensive reading exposes learners to naturally occurring complex sentences far more often than explicit grammar instruction does.
The underlying reason is that repeated exposure allows structures to be acquired implicitly.
In most cases, the effect appears after a full semester of sustained reading.
This claim does not hold, however, in contexts where learners lack access to level-appropriate books.

Rebuild it: write the Warrant

(Claim) Extensive reading programs improve L2 reading fluency more effectively than intensive reading instruction.

(Grounds) Nakanishi (2015) conducted a meta-analysis of 34 studies and found a mean effect size of d = 0.71 in favor of extensive reading on measures of reading speed and comprehension.

(Warrant) [missing]

The paragraph has a Claim and Grounds but no Warrant. Write a Warrant that explains WHY the evidence supports the Claim. If you can, add a Qualifier that limits its scope.

The Warrant explains WHY the evidence supports the Claim (a mechanism or principle, for example implicit acquisition through repeated exposure) instead of repeating the data.
The sentence is written in academic English, connects logically with the Grounds, and includes a Qualifier or hedge (e.g. “likely”, “in most cases”, “tends to”).
Modelo (Apéndice C): «This is likely because extensive reading exposes learners to naturally occurring complex sentences far more often than explicit grammar instruction does, allowing structures to be acquired implicitly through repeated exposure.»

Spot it in the wild

Find an argumentative paragraph in the discussion section of an open-access article in your field. Identify at least three Toulmin components.

  1. Does the paragraph have an explicit Claim in the first sentence, or must the reader infer it?
  2. Do the Grounds include specific data (figures, citations, results), or only general statements?
  3. Is there a Warrant explaining why the Grounds support the Claim, or does the paragraph jump from datum to conclusion?
  4. Do you find any Qualifier or Rebuttal? If not, does the argument sound too categorical?

Not graded. Bring your answers to class and compare them with a classmate.

Now you write

You will write your own paragraph using the Toulmin model pattern. Allow about 15 minutes. Your writing is tracked for academic integrity: write it yourself, in your own words.

Choose a debatable topic from your field and write an argumentative paragraph of five to seven sentences in English that includes at least four of the six Toulmin components. Do not write the labels (Claim, Grounds, Warrant…): the reader must not see them.

General writing criteria
Academic register: formal tone, no contractions or colloquial expressions.
Vocabulary: precise, specific words instead of vague ones (thing, stuff, a lot of).
Cohesion: connectors and reference words link each sentence to the previous one.
Grammar and mechanics: complete sentences, agreement, punctuation, spelling.
Focus: the whole paragraph develops one main idea.
Certainty: hedges and boosters match the strength of the evidence.
