When History Gets It Wrong

Your instructor will introduce the concept of informal logic in historical writing. Pay attention to the difference between how historians argue and how formal logicians argue.

According to Gultekin (2021, p. 44), which of the following best describes informal logic compared to formal logic?

It uses everyday language, pursues pragmatic and pedagogical purposes, and evaluates arguments case by case.
It follows strict binary rules (true/false) and focuses on the form of deduction, not content.
It relies exclusively on mathematical proof and avoids contextual interpretation.
It is used only in natural sciences where experiments can verify claims.
Answer: A — Informal logic uses everyday language and is context-sensitive.
Key quote (p. 44): "Informal logic... uses less and unassertive technical terms, emphasizes daily discussions and arguments more, and pursues a more pragmatic and pedagogical purpose."
Tip: Formal logic = binary (true/false, strict rules). Informal logic = contextual, case-by-case — exactly how historians argue.
Exaggeration
Reductionism
Over-generalization
Presenting a situation, person, or evidence as more important or weighted than it actually is (p. 47).
Treating a person, situation, or evidence as inferior, as if it had no real impact (p. 48).
Applying points belonging to part of a group or situation to the entire group or all situations (p. 49).
Answers:
- Exaggeration: "more important than it is" (e.g., "the most justified war in the world").
- Reductionism: opposite of exaggeration — treating as inferior (e.g., Bayezid II labeled "bad sultan" because territorial expansion was less).
- Over-generalization: part-to-whole error (e.g., assuming steppe conditions were difficult for people who lived there all their lives).
Tip: Exaggeration and Reductionism are mirror opposites — one inflates, the other deflates.

The label "Self-indulgence period" applied to the Ottoman Tulip Era is identified as an Ad Hominem fallacy (p. 48). Why?

It uses a biased label to highlight only one negative aspect of the period, attacking its image rather than analyzing the full historical evidence.
It compares the Tulip Era with a structurally different historical period, creating a false analogy.
It applies a single cause (self-indulgence) to explain the entire Ottoman decline, ignoring other factors.
It is a statistically unfounded claim with no documentary evidence from the period.
Answer: A — biased labeling = Ad Hominem in historiography.
Key quote (p. 48): "With this statement, only one aspect of the period is pointed out, labeling is made, and in this respect, it reflects the Ad Hominem fallacy."
Tip: Ad Hominem in history is NOT only about attacking a person — it includes biased labeling of events or periods to make them look negative deliberately. Note: the author also says this example simultaneously shows stereotypical thinking.
"The Qadi is like the judge in our days."
"The Ottoman Empire entered WWI only because its leaders admired Germany."
Teaching the National Pact (Misak-i Milli) as meaning today's national borders.
False Analogy — the comparison implies the two roles are equivalent at all points, leading to historical misunderstanding (p. 50).
Faulty Causality — a complex historical event is explained with a single cause, ignoring all other factors (p. 51).
Stereotypical Thinking — a cliche interpretation is used without questioning its accuracy or original meaning (p. 52).
Answers:
- Qadi = judge: False Analogy (p. 50) — "leads to a complete misunderstanding".
- WWI + admiration: Faulty Causality (p. 51) — ignores economic, strategic, and political factors.
- National Pact: Stereotypical Thinking (p. 52) — original meaning = national oath, not today's borders.
Tip: False Analogy = wrong comparison between two things. Faulty Causality = wrong reason assigned to an event.

Complete using the author's exact reasoning (pp. 47, 53):

According to Gultekin (p. 47), the term "the most" must be used with caution because it involves a tendency to make something look ___ than it actually is, placing it at the ___.

Similarly, the phrase "the first known" is preferred over simply "the first" because it signals that the claim is qualified by the ___ discovered so far, not as an absolute truth (p. 53).

Answers: greater / top / sources.
Key quote (p. 47): "The term 'the most' must be used with caution in history... it is a term inclined to include fallacies."
Key quote (p. 53): "The phrase 'the first known' is used... to underline that it is qualified as the first based on the sources discovered so far."
Academic writing tip: Avoid absolute superlatives in essays. Replace "the most important battle" with "one of the most significant battles" or "arguably the most decisive engagement."

According to the article (p. 51), which of the following is the core error in a criteria-based fallacy?

An evaluation is made based on the opinion of someone who is not an expert in the specific field being judged, even if they are an expert in another field.
Historical data is used selectively to support a pre-existing conclusion, ignoring contradictory evidence.
A judgment is presented as universal when it only applies to a specific culture or time period.
An emotional narrative is used to make a historical claim more persuasive than it logically deserves to be.
Answer: A — wrong expert = wrong criteria.
Key quote (p. 51): "people, who are not an expert in the field in question even if they are experts in their fields, are listened to make an evaluation."
Example from text (p. 49): Jacques Chirac's statement about shared origins used as historical authority — but a president is not a historian, and his words do not bind academic historians like Coppeaux.
Tip: In academic writing, always evaluate the domain expertise of your sources, not just their general prestige or fame.
Gultekin (2021) argues that, prior to this study, there was no established common classification of fallacies specifically designed for the discipline of history (p. 46).
According to the article, Ad Hominem fallacies in historical reasoning always involve a direct personal attack on the historian or author who produced the source (p. 48).
The author claims that fallacies appear more frequently in digital historical publications than in traditional printed academic journals (p. 53).
Answers:
1. True: p. 46 — explicitly presented as a new contribution to the field.
2. False: p. 48 — Ad Hominem includes labeling events and periods, not only personal attacks.
3. Not Given: no comparison between digital and print media anywhere in the article.
IELTS tip: "Not Given" is NOT the same as "False". False = the text contradicts the statement. Not Given = the text simply does not address it.
