SPRE: Problem and Solution Paragraphs

PEEL builds a paragraph around a claim and its proof. SPRE builds it around a problem and the answer to it: Situation, Problem, Response, Evaluation. Proposed by Michael Hoey, it is the default pattern of article introductions in engineering and applied sciences, wherever a text must justify why something was done.

The model

This is the full abstract of an open-access article on energy poverty in Latin America. It compresses the four components in a single paragraph.

Many of the energy systems functioning across the developing world are poorly designed and largely inadequate to meet a country’s commercial and domestic energy needs. This is especially so in Latin America, where poverty trends have been exacerbated by limited access to energy, which could be used towards supporting industrial and small-scale commercial activities. This has characterised the widespread trend of energy poverty. This phenomenon poses a major barrier towards achieving the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and addressing climate change, since lack of access to reliable energy generation systems hinders the development of economic activities and limits the comfort of living conditions. This paper reports on a study aimed at catering to a better understanding of the challenges related to energy poverty in Latin America and identifying some of the tools that may be deployed to address them. The methodological procedures used were bibliometric analysis and survey. The main findings of this research evidence that reducing energy poverty in Latin American countries is crucial to alleviating household costs since a considerable part of family income is used to pay the energy needs.
Eustachio, J. H. P. P. (2024). Addressing the challenges posed by energy poverty in Latin American countries. Discover Sustainability, 5, Article 262. https://doi.org/10.1007/s43621-024-00426-9

Why SPRE works

SPRE reproduces the most basic narrative structure there is: something was fine, something broke, someone acted, and in the end we know whether the action worked. That sequence is so natural that the reader follows it effortlessly, even when the content is technical.

When a text opens with the problem without placing the reader first, or presents the solution before establishing the problem, reading becomes confusing, even if the data are correct.

PEEL and SPRE do not compete. PEEL organises a paragraph around a claim; SPRE organises a paragraph (or a whole section) around a problem. An empirical article may use SPRE in the introduction to justify the study and PEEL in the discussion to interpret each finding.

Rebuild it: restore the arc

These four sentences form a SPRE paragraph, but they are out of order. Drag them into Situation → Problem → Response → Evaluation.

Indoor air quality in rural households that rely on biomass cookstoves poses a persistent health risk, particularly for women and children.
Despite government subsidies for cleaner fuels, more than 40% of rural households in Ecuador continue to use firewood or charcoal as their primary cooking fuel.
A field trial in three communities in the highlands of Chimborazo province replaced traditional open-fire stoves with improved cookstoves equipped with chimneys.
The monitoring campaign revealed that PM2.5 concentrations exceeded WHO guidelines on 43% of measured days, confirming that the intervention reduced exposure but did not eliminate the health risk entirely.

Spot it in the wild

Find the abstract of an empirical article from your field in open access. Abstracts often compress the four SPRE components into one paragraph.

  1. Does the abstract start by placing the reader in a context (Situation), or does it jump straight to the problem?
  2. Is the Problem phrased as a lack, a contradiction or an unanswered question?
  3. Does the Response name what the study did, or does it only announce what it found?
  4. Does the Evaluation judge the result (positive, partial, negative), or does it simply report it?

Not graded. Bring your answers to class and compare them with a classmate.

Si falta algún componente, discutir si su ausencia debilita la justificación del estudio.

Phrase bank: SPRE

Save the phrases you want to reuse. They go to your personal notebook.

[Field/Topic] has become increasingly relevant in [context]. In [region/discipline], [topic] plays a central role in [process]. [Activity/Practice] is widely recognized as essential for [outcome].
However, [gap/limitation] remains a significant challenge. Despite these advances, little is known about [specific aspect]. Yet [negative condition] continues to affect [population/system].
To address this gap, the present study [verb] ... This paper proposes [solution/approach] as a means of [goal]. A [method/intervention] was implemented in [context] to [purpose].
The results suggest that [response] effectively [outcome]. Preliminary findings indicate that [measure] improved by [amount]. While the intervention showed promise, [limitation] warrants further investigation.

Now you write

You will write your own paragraph using the SPRE (Situation, Problem, Response, Evaluation) pattern. Allow about 12 minutes. Your writing is tracked for academic integrity: write it yourself, in your own words.

Think of a real problem in your professional field or community. Write in English a four-sentence SPRE paragraph that justifies the need to investigate or intervene.
